Ethelred's Folly

The Untouched Wilds sat isolated in a far corner of the world.

Protected to the north by a picturesque yet foreboding range of high mountains, and isolated to the south by the endless sea, the Untouched Wilds enjoyed fair weather and relative peace. The sea breeze pushed inland up the mountain slopes, keeping the air fresh and cool and feeding the rains atop the mountains, which filled the gentle streams and brooks that babbled through the land. Gentle hills rolled across the plain between the mountains and the sea, and wild creatures roamed the land, unmolested by the men of the world.

Such an idyllic time, however, cannot last forever. Eventually, men began to arrive. With picks and shovels, they excavated rude roads that crisscrossed the mountain faces, conquering the high peaks that had so long protected the Untouched Wilds. In the minds of men, the Untouched Wilds were ripe for conquest. They came marching down from the mountains like ants, establishing camps on the hilltops. The camps soon became forts, surrounded by palisades, and the men began to fight among themselves over the bounty of the land. When not warring, they spoiled the land. They chopped trees, hunted animals, and devoured the fruits and berries of the land like a plague of locusts.

The men thought the land unoccupied, not realizing that unseen beings had claimed the Untouched Wilds long before their arrival. The invasion of men was watched with a cautious eye by many spirits of the land: sprites, faeries, dryads, and other creatures of the Faewild. Word of the sins of the men soon reached the great and terrible King of the Fae, who had stewarded the land since its creation. The King was most displeased, and made plans to evict the invaders.

So it was that the torment of men began. On the King's orders, the Fae poisoned the dreams of the men. They whispered cruel thoughts into their ears as they slept. Some men awakened to vanishing shadows in their camps, finding small items missing and large items misplaced. Other men began sleepwalking. The fortunate ones would awaken in treetops or brooks; the unfortunate ones would sleepwalk into the sea or the mountains, never to return.

Those on watch, or those who just could not sleep, often saw shambling beasts emerging from the woods, sniffing at the gates and clawing at the palisades, only to have the beasts vanish when the alarm was raised. Strange lights streaked across the night skies. The stars pulsed and shifted in an unnatural way. Shooting stars tore fast and loud through the sky, careening over the encampments only to climb over the mountains and rocket away into the night.

Daylight offered no respite. Strange creatures flitted about in the shadows. Men, women, and children felt like they were being watched. Paranoia became rampant, and the men began fighting among themselves. Women and children, frightened and disoriented, walked into the wilderness and simply vanished.

Eventually, the haunting became too much for the men. Most men deemed the land cursed and walked back across the mountains, abandoning the Untouched Wilds.

One chieftain, however, refused to be swayed. The Great Warlord Cuthbert the Red, tall, handsome, and red-haired, gave fiery speeches to his tribe, convincing them to stay in this land. They were strong; the others were weak. They would endure where others gave up. Cuthbert's tribe would not be cowed by the Fae: they would fight the wicked spirits of the land with their whole being.

Cuthbert fortified his hilltop fortress, adding layers of stone around the palisades. He had his men cast a large iron arch and mount heavy oak doors to his fortress. His men cleared the land around the hill, piled up defensive berms, and lit large fires at night.

Worse still, Cuthbert began to patrol the land. He and his men began to fight back against the unseen defenders. They invoked powerful spirits to help them conquer the land, and soon learned how to spot the Fae. They attacked with iron arrowheads and iron swords, imbued with dark magic and forged under the watchful eye of the tribe's holy man and the spirit he had summoned, a lion-headed being who helped the men improve their forts and their weapons.

Knowing that war has no victors, only survivors, the King of the Fae sent a messenger to Cuthbert. “The King of the Fae wishes to parley,” the messenger said. “While the other men fled, you remained. When they gave up, you endured. The King recognizes you as strong and worthy among men, and wishes to speak to you as one royal to another.” Cautiously, Cuthbert accepted. He and his men were told to circle a small hill three times, and on so doing, they found themselves in the Fae city of Nocturne, where the messenger escorted them to the King's hall.

The King received Cuthbert as royalty, paying tribute with fine clothes and enchanting wines. He then made his offer.

“You, Cuthbert the Red, are a rarity among men. We find you to be a formidable chief, a born leader, a tenacious opponent, and a worthy ruler of the land. I offer you a treaty: I will cede control of the fields, harbor, and mountain passes to you, keeping only the western woods for my people. We will not war or interfere, nor will we harass or bother. We will recede to the woods and respect your sovereignty, for as long as your bloodline rules over the land.”

Cuthbert accepted.

In celebration, the King held a vast and splendid banquet. Cuthbert and his men were toasted and given succulent food and fine wine. The Fae entertained the men with beautiful magic and wild tales. After much wine had flowed, the Fae offered the men board for the night and a final sendoff in the morning. As the men slept, the Fae princess Titania came to their quarters and seduced Cuthbert, charming him into her bed.

The following morning, Cuthbert and his men were hosted at a fine breakfast. They were served tea, crisp and savory bacon, and delectable sweet rolls, drizzled with rich honey. After breakfast, the King and Cuthbert signed the treaty, and the men were escorted back to the land.

Once back on familiar ground, the men began to feel strange. One at a time, they fell to their knees. They screamed in agony as their bones lengthened, their noses grew, and their bodies sprouted thick brown hair. They stood on their hind legs and roared, transforming from proud men into massive bears. Betrayed and confused, knowing that their fellows would hunt them down if they went back to the fortress, they roamed the land and drifted into the wilderness.

The people knew none of this. Their leader, their chieftain, had simply vanished. Leaderless and abandoned, the people were uneasy. They lived in fear, and soon factions began to bicker and quarrel. Cuthbert's land entered a period of chaos and darkness lasting 21 years. Many men left, but some stayed, working the land and keeping the settlement as best they could. The Fae kept their word: they did not intervene, nor did they torment or tease the men.

At the end of the 21st year of chaos, a delegation arrived at Cuthbert's fort. They arrived on horseback, led by a tall, handsome, red-haired man. The elders thought it to be Cuthbert himself, but the man introduced himself as Cuthbert's son by the Fae princess Titania, and said his name was Harald New-Dawn. He was now the rightful ruler of the land. A few men challenged Cuthbert, but he dispatched them quickly and mercilessly, establishing himself as the undisputed ruler of the land.

Harald's people prospered under strong leadership, and a period of relative calm followed.

Until, that is, two knights arrived at the fort with a dozen men. They rode across the mountains, mounted on white horses and wearing gleaming armor. The first knight wore a shock of red hair and piercing blue eyes. The other had the head of a lion and gleaming silver armor. The red-haired knight announced to all present that his name was Ethelred, and that he was the long-lost firstborn son of Cuthbert. His visage proved his ancestry; his paperwork proved his age; and he was therefore, under the ancient traditions of men, entitled to the throne.

Harald refused to stand down. “Brothers should not spill one another's blood, and the long traditions of our people agree with you. But I have ruled this kingdom for many years, and brought it from the brink of abandon to prosperity and safety, and I will not abandon my people. To avoid violence and bloodshed, let us parley on uncontested ground, where the contract of our bloodline was inked, in the halls of my mother's people.”

Harald, Ethelred, and his men rode to the small hill and circled it, finding themselves in Nocturne. They journeyed to the Fae King's hall, and were soon seated around a banquet. As they feasted, the King questioned them both. Having heard them out, he addressed them.

“Ethelred, Harald. I speak to you as King and cosigner. Many years ago I inked my name to an agreement with Cuthbert that his bloodline should always rule the land. My sources tell me that it is true: Ethelred is the firstborn son, and by right, the land is his.” Harald, taken aback, frowned. The lion-headed knight grinned smugly.

“However, I sense in you, Ethelred, the same greatness I sensed in your father. Harald is a good king, and a good ruler, but he is not a good conqueror. He is not as ambitious as you. As beautiful as the land is, it is remote, isolated, and impossible to expand. Surely, Ethelred, you would prefer a land from which you could launch a conquest and live to your greatness.”

Ethelred pondered this. His lion-headed companion, however, was outraged. “I object!” he screamed. “Ethelred has every right to this land. How can the King promise him greatness in another place over which neither you nor he has dominion?” His thunderous outburst echoed through the hall, and an uncomfortable silence settled in.

The King sat back in his throne and looked upward, thinking. He spoke softly. “Ethelred desires conquest, my feline friend. We both sense it. Harald has stewarded the land well, and his people need him, and they are unsuitable for military life.” Ethelred now wore a frown. The King stood, spread his arms, and spoke more loudly now.

“Ethelred, I am a man of my word. Cuthbert's blood must rule the land. But my people's trickery has never been repaid. I offer you this: for reparations to your bloodline, I gift you a wagonload of the kingdom's gold and diamonds, enough to buy allies and build castles. I also gift you safe passage to the Golden Forest, and, as a token of my esteem and my honor, this amulet of my people.” Opening a small box, the King produced an ancient Fae amulet, a beautiful necklace made of pure gold, with a large ruby at its heart. “This amulet, Ethelred, is charmed with the magic of my people. Whoever wears it becomes irresistible to the most desirable wenches and the most buxom sows. Your conquests need not be only of the land. It is your choice: if you wish to take your rightful inheritance, Harald will give up his rule. But if conquest and dominion is your true desire, I give you the gifts you need to make it so. Either way, we honor the pact made with your father, Cuthbert.”

The lion-headed knight snorted loudly. “No deal!” he snarled. “The boy wants—”

Ethelred stood. “I take your offer. Harald may keep his backwater kingdom. I shall pursue glory in the name of my father.” He extended a hand, and the King swept in and shook it. “The deal is done,” he said.

Hours later, before the hall, Ethelred and his men were mounted atop their horses. Behind them stood a wagon containing six chests of gold and diamonds. Ethelred's breast was decorated with the amulet. The Fae King stepped out of his hall and addressed the men.

“Ethelred, son of Cuthbert, I honor our pact. Your treasure is behind you, and now…” he struck the doorway of his hall with a small golden tuning fork, “your safe passage is before you. This doorway will lead you to the edge of the Golden Forest. There are many tribes of men near the forest for you to conquer, be it with money or might. Go now and seek your destiny.”

Ethelred and his men spurred their horses and charged through the portal. The lion-headed knight, however, simply glared at the King. After watching Ethelred and his men enter the portal, he uttered a brief curse.

“Damn you, Fae King! You've ruined it all!”

With startling speed, he then charged the King, snatched the tuning fork from his hands, and disappeared in a puff of smoke. The portal closed behind the last of Ethelred's men; Harald returned to his kingdom, and the Fae King to his hall.

Emerging from the portal into the Golden Forest, Ethelred and his men galloped on, then slowed. The forest was thick. Ethelred signaled his men and they came to a stop. They were on the edge of the forest, but it was the edge against the mountains. They were deeper in the woods than Ethelred had thought.

Suddenly, one of the men cried out. The chests, previously full of gleaming coins and sparking diamonds, were now full of clods of dirt and small pebbles. Ethelred's heart sank, then began to pound as a deafening roar echoed through the woods. Another roar followed, and then another. Bears—at least a dozen of them—appeared in the woods. They stood on their hind legs, sniffing the air. Ethelred and his men began to close ranks, back to back, weapons drawn. Without warning, the bears charged. Horses bucked, and men fell to the ground. Blood spilled. Ethelred swung his sword, cutting into a bear. The injured bear stepped back, but before he could land a killing blow, another bear cuffed Ethelred, knocking him to the ground and making him drop his weapon. He lay in the dirt, dazed, hearing the shrieks of his men around him. A shadow loomed before him, and he saw powerful jaws flashing in the filtered light of the woods.

As he felt the powerful jaw bite into his neck, his last recollection was that a female bear was often known as a sow.
